# Plan: Graph-Based Discovery Dynamics in the Adjacent Possible

## Overview

This research program treats the Mathlib theorem dependency graph as a constraint-based possibility space where mathematical discovery proceeds through exploration of the "adjacent possible" - theorems that become provable given current knowledge. Unlike models of discovery as free exploration, this approach recognizes that mathematical accessibility is determined by hard logical constraints: a theorem cannot be discovered until all its prerequisite theorems exist in the knowledge base. The DAG structure in our data represents ground truth about these dependencies, enabling postdictive analysis of what could and could not have been discovered at each stage of mathematical development. Our goal is to quantify discovery difficulty not as search complexity but as structural properties of how the possibility space expands and contracts during exploration.

## Experiment 1: Dynamics of the Adjacent Possible

The fundamental question is how the space of discoverable-but-not-yet-discovered theorems evolves as knowledge accumulates, and whether this evolution exhibits phase transitions, bottlenecks, or explosive expansions that characterize periods of mathematical fertility versus stagnation. We define the adjacent possible at time t as A_t = {theorems whose prerequisites are all in the known set K_t, but which are not themselves in K_t}, and we track how |A_t| evolves under different discovery strategies including breadth-first search, random exploration, and greedy expansion that maximizes |A_{t+1}| at each step. We measure the growth curve |A_t| versus t, the volatility (rate of change in |A_t|), and the distribution of "expansion events" where discovering a single theorem causes large jumps in |A_t|. If we observe periods where |A_t| grows exponentially, this indicates mathematically fertile periods where each discovery unlocks many others, whereas stagnation periods with declining |A_t| suggest the explorer has reached a dead-end region of the graph requiring backtracking or external insight. This would reveal that discovery difficulty is not uniform but depends critically on the current position in knowledge space - some regions are rich with possibilities while others are sparse, and different strategies may get trapped in different local regions. Conversely, if |A_t| grows smoothly and monotonically, this would suggest the mathematical possibility space expands uniformly and discovery order matters little, which would be a surprising finding given the hierarchical nature of mathematical knowledge.

## Experiment 2: Temporal Accessibility Versus Discovery

A theorem's accessibility time - when it first enters the adjacent possible - is distinct from when it is actually discovered, and the gap between these moments reveals how "obvious" or "hidden" a theorem is once it becomes provable. We compute for each theorem T the minimal depth in the DAG (accessibility time under optimal BFS), the expected discovery time under random walk, and the variance in discovery time across multiple random exploration runs, then examine theorems with large gaps between accessibility and average discovery. Theorems that enter A_t early but are discovered late (high variance, long mean discovery time relative to accessibility time) are "hiding in plain sight" - they are provable but non-obvious, requiring either lucky exploration or strategic search to identify among many possibilities. We measure the size |A_t| at the moment when each theorem T first enters the adjacent possible, which we term the "dilution factor" - theorems entering when |A_t| is large are diluted among many options and harder to find. If we discover that high-impact theorems (measured by your existing MDL gain metric or by betweenness centrality) have low dilution factors, this would suggest important theorems are discoverable at moments when few alternatives exist, making them "forced moves" that any explorer would likely find. Alternatively, if high-impact theorems have high dilution factors, this would indicate that significant mathematical advances require identifying the right theorem among hundreds of accessible options, suggesting discovery difficulty lies not in making theorems accessible but in recognizing their value.

## Experiment 3: Pathway Diversity and Bottleneck Structure

Mathematical knowledge may have unique gateways - theorems whose discovery is necessary to access entire regions of downstream results - versus robust theorems reachable through many different prior discovery sequences, and identifying these bottlenecks reveals the dependency structure of mathematical progress. For each theorem T, we compute the number of distinct minimal hitting sets of its prerequisites (minimal sets of theorems that collectively cover all of T's dependencies), and we measure the "removal impact" by computing what fraction of downstream theorems become unreachable if T is removed from the available knowledge. Theorems with few hitting sets are fragile, requiring a specific sequence of prior discoveries, while those with many hitting sets are robust, discoverable from multiple directions. We create a histogram of removal impacts across all theorems to identify the distribution of "gateway" theorems and examine whether high-removal-impact theorems correlate with high betweenness centrality or high MDL gain. If we find that a small number of theorems (say 1% of the graph) account for access to the majority of downstream results, this would indicate mathematical knowledge has a bow-tie structure with critical intermediate abstractions that must be discovered for progress, suggesting that discovery difficulty is dominated by whether you find these gateways rather than by the total number of theorems. If instead removal impacts are uniformly distributed, this would suggest mathematical knowledge is robustly interconnected with many alternative paths to any result, making the order of discovery relatively unimportant for ultimate coverage.

## Experiment 4: Crystallization Patterns as Adjacent Possible Templates

Your existing work on premise co-occurrence patterns (crystallization) identified that certain premise combinations like {mul_assoc, mul_comm} appear together in hundreds of theorems, suggesting these patterns represent families of related results that likely become accessible as a coordinated group when their common prerequisites are met. We identify all theorems matching each of your top-K crystallization patterns and track when these theorem families first enter the adjacent possible, measuring whether they enter as atomic clusters (all members become accessible within a small time window) or whether they dribble into A_t gradually over extended periods. For each pattern P, we measure the "family coherence time" - the time span between when the first and last members of the pattern-family enter A_t - and we examine whether discovering one member of a family makes other members more likely to be discovered next (measured by conditional discovery probabilities in random walk simulations). If families enter A_t atomically with short coherence times, this would indicate that crystallization patterns are natural units of mathematical possibility, with entire families becoming accessible together when their shared prerequisites are met, suggesting these patterns are the "natural abstractions" in the space and discovery strategies should target them explicitly. Conversely, if family members enter A_t scattered across time, this would suggest that premise co-occurrence is a post-hoc pattern visible only after discovery, not a structural feature of the possibility space, which would weaken the interpretation of crystallization patterns as fundamental units.

## Experiment 5: Strategy Comparison and Human Discovery Order

Different exploration strategies should lead to different trajectories through the adjacent possible, and comparing these trajectories to actual Mathlib development history provides validation of which strategies capture human-like mathematical discovery behavior. We implement four strategies: (1) BFS optimal expansion discovering theorems as soon as accessible, (2) random walk uniformly sampling from A_t at each step, (3) greedy expansion selecting the theorem from A_t that maximizes |A_{t+1}|, and (4) premise-cluster following that preferentially discovers theorems matching previously seen crystallization patterns. We run each strategy to full graph coverage (or timeout) and measure the discovery time distribution for all theorems, the total steps required for complete coverage, and the trajectory of |A_t| over time. We then extract the approximate chronological development order from Mathlib commit history (using file creation dates and dependency analysis) to obtain a proxy for human discovery order, and measure the correlation between human discovery times and each strategy's discovery times. If greedy expansion or premise-cluster following matches human discovery order better than random walk, this would validate that human mathematicians use strategic heuristics to navigate the adjacent possible rather than exploring uniformly, and would identify which heuristics are most predictive. If random walk matches human order equally well, this would be a profound finding suggesting that despite extensive training and strategic intent, mathematical discovery is effectively random exploration constrained only by accessibility, implying that the graph structure dominates over human choice.

## Experiment 6: Memory-Constrained Discovery and Phase Transitions

Real mathematical agents (humans or bounded computational systems) cannot maintain perfect recall of all previously encountered theorems, and imposing memory constraints on discovery agents may reveal fundamental limitations on what can be discovered with bounded cognitive resources. We modify the discovery rule such that a theorem T enters A_t only if all its prerequisites are within the agent's memory window of the last K discovered theorems, where K ranges from 5 to 500, and we measure what fraction of the full graph becomes discoverable at each K value. For each theorem T, we compute the "critical memory K_c(T)" - the minimum memory required for any strategy to discover T - and we examine the distribution of K_c across all theorems. We run random walk simulations at each K value and measure coverage (fraction of theorems ever discovered), rediscovery rate (how often theorems are visited multiple times as prerequisites cycle in and out of memory), and discovery time distributions. If we observe a sharp phase transition at some critical K* where coverage jumps from near-zero to near-complete, this would indicate that mathematical discovery has a fundamental memory complexity threshold, and theorems requiring K > K* represent a qualitatively different difficulty class requiring either extended working memory or external memory aids (notes, papers). If coverage increases smoothly with K, this would suggest memory constraints create a continuous difficulty spectrum rather than discrete complexity classes, and any memory size enables discovery of some fixed fraction of mathematics.

## Experiment 7: MDL Gain and Adjacent Possible Expansion

High MDL gain theorems compress the description of many downstream proofs, suggesting they might also expand the adjacent possible more than low-MDL theorems by serving as powerful abstractions that make many subsequent theorems accessible. We partition theorems into quintiles by their MDL gain values from your existing analysis, and for each quintile we measure the average "expansion factor" - the increase in |A_t| immediately after discovering theorems in that quintile normalized by the pre-discovery size. We also measure the "downstream accessibility impact" by computing for each theorem T how many additional theorems enter A_{t+1}, A_{t+2}, ..., A_{t+10} in the 10 steps following T's discovery under BFS, capturing both immediate and delayed expansion effects. We examine whether high-MDL theorems have systematically higher expansion factors, and whether theorems with negative MDL gain (your finding that 99.5% have negative gain) have near-zero expansion factors, indicating they are "leaf nodes" in the conceptual structure. If high-MDL theorems consistently expand A_t by 10-100x while low-MDL theorems expand by 1-2x, this would confirm that compression and generativity are unified - theorems that compress proofs also generate possibilities - suggesting MDL gain is not just a measure of usefulness but of structural importance in organizing the possibility space. If we find no correlation between MDL gain and expansion factor, this would indicate that compression and accessibility are orthogonal properties, with some theorems being highly compressive but not generative, forcing us to distinguish between "abstractive" theorems (high MDL) and "gateway" theorems (high expansion).

## Experiment 8: Depth-Adjusted Discovery Difficulty

Graph depth (topological distance from axioms) provides a baseline measure of discovery difficulty - deeper theorems require more prior discoveries - but depth alone may not capture true difficulty if some depth-N theorems are accessible through many paths while others require specific narrow sequences. We compute for each theorem T its depth d(T) in the DAG and its discovery time under various strategies, then examine the residual difficulty after accounting for depth by measuring the ratio r(T) = E[discovery_time(T)] / d(T) where expectation is over random walk runs. Theorems with r(T) >> 1 are "harder than their depth suggests" while r(T) ≈ 1 indicates difficulty explained entirely by depth. We identify the top-100 theorems with highest r(T) values and examine their structural properties: do they have unusual premise combinations (high novelty relative to crystallization patterns), unusually many prerequisites (high in-degree), or low betweenness (few paths through them)? If depth-adjusted difficult theorems cluster in specific subgraphs or mathematical domains (e.g., analysis vs algebra), this would indicate that discovery difficulty has domain-specific characteristics beyond structural graph properties, possibly reflecting conceptual distance or the need for domain-specific intuitions. If high-r(T) theorems are uniformly distributed across domains and their difficulty is explained entirely by low pathway diversity or high dilution factors, this would suggest discovery difficulty is universal structural property independent of mathematical content.

## Synthesis: Toward a Quantitative Theory of Mathematical Discovery Difficulty

The adjacent possible framework reframes discovery difficulty from "how hard to search" to "how hard to access and recognize," grounding difficulty in the evolution of the possibility space under logical constraints rather than in computational search complexity. By measuring accessibility times, dilution factors, pathway diversity, expansion dynamics, and their correlations with MDL gain and graph centrality metrics, we construct a multi-dimensional characterization of theorem difficulty that distinguishes between depth (how many steps), fragility (how many paths), dilution (how many alternatives), and generativity (how much expansion). The success of this program would produce a predictive model where given a theorem's structural properties in the DAG and its premise composition, we can estimate both when it becomes accessible and how difficult it is to find once accessible, potentially enabling identification of "undiscovered but accessible" theorems in existing mathematical corpora - results that are provable with current knowledge but remain unnoticed. More fundamentally, if we find strong correlations between these graph-theoretic difficulty measures and actual ATP search costs or human development times, this would validate that the fossil record of mathematical dependencies contains sufficient information to reconstruct discovery dynamics, suggesting that the space of mathematical truths has intrinsic structure that shapes discovery independently of the agents doing the discovering.
